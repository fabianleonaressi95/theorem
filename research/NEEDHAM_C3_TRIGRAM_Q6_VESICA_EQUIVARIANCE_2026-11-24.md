## 2. An order-three linear map on a trigram

Represent a trigram by a vector \(x=(a,b,c)\in\mathbb F_2^3\), with all arithmetic modulo two. Define
\[
T(a,b,c)=(b,a+b,c).
\]
In matrix form,
\[
T=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&1&0\\
1&1&0\\
0&0&1
\end{pmatrix}
\in GL(3,2).
\]
A direct multiplication gives
\[
T^2(a,b,c)=(a+b,a,c),\qquad T^3=I.
\]
Since \(T\ne I\), its order is exactly three.

Its fixed-point set is
\[
\operatorname{Fix}(T)=\{(0,0,0),(0,0,1)\}.
\]
The remaining six states split into two three-cycles:
\[
(010)\to(110)\to(100)\to(010),
\]
\[
(011)\to(111)\to(101)\to(011).
\]
Thus the orbit-size partition is
\[
\boxed{8=1+1+3+3.}
\]
Equivalently, the eight trigrams consist of two fixed states and two free \(C_3\)-orbits.

## 3. Independent actions on the lower and upper trigram

A hexagram is an ordered pair
\[
H=(L,U)\in\mathbb F_2^3\times\mathbb F_2^3.
\]
Let the group
\[
G=C_3\times C_3=\langle T_L\rangle\times\langle T_U\rangle
\]
act independently by
\[
(g_L,g_U)\cdot(L,U)=(g_LL,g_UU).
\]
This is an action of a group of order nine on the 64 hexagrams.

The product-orbit classification follows directly from the trigram orbits:

| Lower orbit | Upper orbit | Number of product orbits | Size of each |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| fixed (2 choices) | fixed (2 choices) | 4 | 1 |
| fixed (2) | 3-cycle (2) | 4 | 3 |
| 3-cycle (2) | fixed (2) | 4 | 3 |
| 3-cycle (2) | 3-cycle (2) | 4 | 9 |

Combining the two middle rows gives the correct distribution:
\[
\boxed{4\cdot1+8\cdot3+4\cdot9=64.}
\]
There are **four** singleton orbits, **eight** orbits of size three, and **four** orbits of size nine. The often tempting expression \(4\cdot1+4\cdot3+4\cdot9\) is incorrect: it accounts for only 52 states and omits four size-three orbits. The product action is independent in the two factors; it is not the diagonal cyclic action generated by \((T,T)\), whose orbit structure would be different.

This classification is exact and purely finite-combinatorial. It refines the factorization
\[
Q_6\cong Q_3^{\rm lower}\square Q_3^{\rm upper}
\]
by a specified subgroup of \(GL(3,2)\times GL(3,2)\). It does not assert that this subgroup acts by Euclidean isometries on the sampled cone arcs.

## 4. Why the current eight Vesica samples are not C3-equivariant

In the two-sheet construction, each trigram is assigned through a bijection
\[
\kappa:\mathbb F_2^3\longrightarrow\{0,1,\ldots,7\}
\]
to one of eight points on a 120-degree arc:
\[
\theta_k=-\frac{\pi}{3}+\frac{2k\pi}{21},
\qquad k=0,\ldots,7.
\]
The second sheet uses the corresponding arc
\[
\varphi_k=\frac{2\pi}{3}+\frac{2k\pi}{21}.
\]
The eight points include both endpoints and form seven equal subintervals.

A proposed equivariance condition for a planar rotation would be
\[
\Phi_3(Tb)=R_{2\pi/3}\Phi_3(b),
\]
where \(\Phi_3\) is the trigram-to-point map and \(R_{2\pi/3}\) is rotation by 120 degrees about a declared centre.

This condition does **not** hold for the existing sampled arcs, and cannot hold as a permutation of those eight points under the ordinary 120-degree rotation about the circle centre: the selected 120-degree arc is not invariant under that rotation. Moreover, the action \(T\) has two distinct fixed trigrams. An injective map into the plane intertwining \(T\) with a nontrivial 120-degree rotation would have to send both fixed trigrams to fixed points of that rotation, but a nontrivial planar rotation has only one fixed point. This is a direct obstruction, independent of how the eight sample labels are assigned.

Therefore:
- assigning the three members of each algebraic orbit to the phases \(1,\omega,\omega^2\) is a valid abstract labelling;
- it is not an equivariant realization on the current single planar circle;
- the two fixed states require additional geometric room (for example, separate sheet/sector labels or an enlarged configuration space), or a non-Euclidean/non-rigid action;
- any proposed replacement action must be written explicitly and its metric properties proved.

The two-sheet Minkowski construction may offer that additional room, but no such equivariant embedding is established in this note.

## 5. Precise common structure with Needham's cubic roots

The correspondence is at the level of cyclic order-three structure:

1. In the complex plane, multiplication by \(\omega\) rotates a nonzero complex number by 120 degrees and cycles the three cube-root phases.
2. In \(\mathbb F_2^3\), \(T\) has order three and cycles two disjoint sets of three trigrams while fixing two other trigrams.
3. On a hexagram, independent actions on the lower and upper trigrams give the product group \(C_3\times C_3\) and the orbit sizes 1, 3 and 9.
4. The Vesica chord and the difference of the two non-real cube roots both have length \(\sqrt3\) at unit scale, as recorded in the Needham/Vesica dossier.

These statements share a mathematical symmetry pattern, but they are not the same representation: multiplication by \(\omega\) on the complex plane has one fixed point (the origin), whereas \(T\) on \(\mathbb F_2^3\) has two fixed states. The fixed-point mismatch is exactly why the naive planar equivariance fails.

## 6. Relation to the Mandelbrot iteration

The quadratic iteration
\[
z_{n+1}=z_n^2+c
\]
does not possess a general threefold rotational symmetry in \(z\) or \(c\). In particular, the transformation \(z\mapsto\omega z\) does not commute with the quadratic map for arbitrary \(c\):
\[
(\omega z)^2+c=\omega^2z^2+c
\]
whereas
\[
\omega(z^2+c)=\omega z^2+\omega c.
\]
These expressions are not equal in general. Special parameter transformations or conjugacies may be investigated separately, but no universal \(C_3\) symmetry of the Mandelbrot iteration follows from the roots-of-unity/Vesica correspondence.

Consequently, this note does not validate any cosmological interpretation or physical claim in the previously discussed Mandelbrot/fractal document. It supplies a finite algebraic symmetry candidate that could be compared with a specified dynamical system only after the action on both state and parameter is defined and checked.
