# Sangaku Geometry: A Classification of Circle Configurations by Symmetry Groups and Algebraic Distance Fields

## Abstract
We propose a classification framework for finite Euclidean configurations generated by successive circle and line constructions, using (i) incidence/tangency structure, (ii) Euclidean symmetry group, and (iii) normalized coordinate and distance fields. The framework is motivated by classical Japanese Sangaku problems, including constructions involving the Vesica Piscis, regular pentagons and mutually tangent circles.

The first theorem establishes a quadratic-tower bound for constructions using only line-line, line-circle and circle-circle intersections, together with circumcircle constructions through three non-collinear constructed points. It does not automatically cover general Apollonius problems, arbitrary tangency systems or nonlinear metric constraints. Historical attribution is treated separately from validity of an algebraic model.

## 1. Configuration data
A finite marked circle configuration is
\[
\mathcal S=(V,\mathcal C,\mathcal L,\mathcal I,\mathcal T,\Gamma),
\]
where V is the set of distinguished points, C the circles, L the lines, I the incidence data, T the tangencies (including internal/external type), and Gamma the directed construction graph recording the operation used for each object.

Marks matter. The symmetry group of an unlabelled picture can be larger than the subgroup preserving all construction labels and distinguished objects.

## 2. Three different algebraic records
After fixing a Euclidean frame and normalization (for example, one distinguished length equal to one), define:
- K_coord: field generated over the base field by all point coordinates;
- K_sq: field generated by squared distances between distinguished points and squared radii;
- K_dist: field generated by corresponding nonnegative distances and radii.

These fields need not coincide. Squared distances can lie in a smaller field than distances. A change of Euclidean frame can enlarge a raw coordinate field, so comparisons require a declared normalization or invariant formulation.

The Euclidean symmetry group G_Euc(S) consists of isometries preserving the marked configuration. Record the similarity group separately when scale is forgotten. Neither group is identified with a field automorphism group without a separately proved action.

## 3. Quadratic-tower theorem

**Theorem 1 (quadratic tower for intersection constructions).** Let K0 be a subfield of R of characteristic zero. Start with finitely many points, lines and circles whose defining coefficients belong to K0. Suppose every new point is obtained by a finite sequence of:
1. line-line intersections;
2. line-circle intersections;
3. circle-circle intersections.

A circle through three already constructed non-collinear points may also be added at any stage. Then every constructed point has coordinates in a finite tower
\[
K_0\subseteq K_1\subseteq\cdots\subseteq K_m,\qquad [K_{j+1}:K_j]\leq 2.
\]
In particular, every constructed coordinate is expressible by nested square roots over K0, and the construction is compass-and-straightedge constructible from the initial data.

**Proof.** Assume inductively that all current coordinates lie in a field K. A line-line intersection is obtained by solving a nonsingular 2x2 linear system, so its coordinates lie in K. A line-circle intersection is obtained by parametrizing the line and substituting into the circle equation; this gives a polynomial of degree at most two over K. Its real solutions therefore lie in an extension of degree at most two. For two circles, subtracting their equations eliminates the common quadratic terms and gives their radical axis, a line over K. Their intersection points are then obtained from a line-circle quadratic. Finally, the centre of the circle through three non-collinear points is the solution of two linear perpendicular-bisector equations over K; its squared radius is in K. Thus the induction closes. A finite construction uses finitely many such steps, giving the stated tower. QED.

**Scope.** The theorem concerns intersection constructions. It does not assert that every system of circle tangencies is quadratic. A general Apollonius construction must be reduced and analysed separately; eliminating variables can produce higher-degree equations, and degeneracies must be handled explicitly.

## 4. Case study A — Vesica Piscis
Take two equal circles of radius r, with centres A=(0,0), B=(s,0), where 0<s<2r. Their common points satisfy
\[
x^2+y^2=r^2,\qquad (x-s)^2+y^2=r^2.
\]
Subtracting yields x=s/2, hence
\[
P_\pm=(s/2,\pm\sqrt{r^2-s^2/4}),\qquad L=|P_+P_-|=\sqrt{4r^2-s^2}.
\]
For r=s=1, the points are (1/2, ±sqrt(3)/2), and L=sqrt(3). If r,s are rational, the coordinate and distance extensions are contained in Q(sqrt(4r^2-s^2)); the squared common-chord length is rational.

For the unlabelled two-circle lens together with its two centres and two intersection points, the full isometry group is the order-four group generated by reflection in the centre line and reflection in the perpendicular bisector (isomorphic to C2 x C2). If centres or circles are individually labelled, only label-preserving symmetries remain.

## 5. Case study B — regular pentagon
Let a regular pentagon have side l, diagonal d, and circumradius R. Then
\[
d/l=\varphi=(1+\sqrt5)/2,\qquad \varphi^2=\varphi+1,
\]
and
\[
l^2=(5-\sqrt5)R^2/2,\qquad d^2=(5+\sqrt5)R^2/2.
\]
Thus the field generated by squared characteristic distances, with R=1, is Q(sqrt(5)).

For vertices z_k=zeta_5^k, the complex coordinate field is Q(zeta_5), of degree four. Real Cartesian coordinates require adjoining i, so a convenient field containing them is Q(zeta_20)=Q(zeta_5,i), of degree eight over Q. The real field generated by the Cartesian coordinates is
\[
Q(\sqrt5,\sqrt{10+2\sqrt5}),
\]
of degree four. The Euclidean symmetry group is D5 of order ten. The Galois group of Q(zeta_5)/Q is cyclic of order four; it is not D5.

A Sangaku construction diagram involving a pentagon must be transcribed object-by-object before claiming that this abstract pentagon field is the field generated by that particular construction.

## 6. Case study C — equal-radius outcome in a tangency model
Consider the dimensionless algebraic model
\[
r_1=(r^2-x^2)/(2r),\quad
r_3=-r_1+\sqrt{r_1^2+x^2},\quad
r_4=(r^2+x^2)/(2r)-r_1.
\]
For r>0 and 0<=x<=r,
\[
\sqrt{r_1^2+x^2}=(r^2+x^2)/(2r),
\]
because the right-hand side is nonnegative and its square equals the radicand. Consequently
\[
r_3=r_4=x^2/r.
\]
For x/r=1/3, this gives r1/r=4/9 and r3/r=r4/r=1/9.

This is an exact algebraic identity for the displayed model. It is not yet certified as a reconstruction of a specific 1837 Sangaku: the original tablet/diagram, circle labels, and each internal/external tangency condition must be checked before historical attribution.

## 7. Case study D — Hirano's Sangaku pentagon construction

Sparavigna and Baldi's 2016 digital Sangaku article transcribes a construction attributed to Yosifusa Hirano: perpendicular diameters AB and CD of a unit circle; circles with diameters AO and BO; the intersection T of line CQ with the circle centred at Q; and the circle centred at C with radius CT. Its intersections E,F with the unit circle are adjacent vertices of a regular pentagon.

In the normalized frame O=(0,0), A=(-1,0), B=(1,0), C=(0,1), D=(0,-1), P=(-1/2,0), Q=(1/2,0), exact calculation gives
\[
CT=(\sqrt5-1)/2,\quad
E,F=\left(\pm\frac{\sqrt{10-2\sqrt5}}4,\frac{1+\sqrt5}{4}\right).
\]
Consequently
\[
|EF|^2=(5-\sqrt5)/2,\qquad E\cdot F=(\sqrt5-1)/4=\cos(72^\circ).
\]
Thus the source construction is verified as an exact Euclidean pentagon construction. The coordinate implementation and regression test are in \`hirano_sangaku.py\` and \`tests/test_hirano_sangaku.py\`; the source transcription and its visual-inspection limitation are recorded in SGC-004 and SOURCE_REGISTER.md. The paper's pixel-based digital rendering is not used as the mathematical proof.

## 7. Classification questions
1. Which finite list of combinatorial, metric and field invariants classifies marked circle configurations up to congruence or similarity?
2. When does a geometric symmetry group constrain the coordinate-field or distance-field Galois action?
3. Which tangency-only construction systems remain within quadratic towers, and which produce higher algebraic degree?
4. Can a source-verified Sangaku corpus be encoded so that diagrams, equations, construction graphs and proof status are machine-checkable?
