## 2. Contact data and conventions

Let (M) be a three-dimensional co-oriented contact manifold. Fix a contact form (	heta), so
[
	hetawedge d	heta\ne0,
qquad H=\ker\theta.
]
Let (T) be the Reeb vector field:
[
\theta(T)=1,\qquad \iota_Td\theta=0.
]

In a Darboux chart use
[
\theta=dt+\frac12(x\,dy-y\,dx),\qquad
d\theta=dx\wedge dy,
]
and the horizontal frame
[
X=\partial_x+\frac y2\partial_t,\qquad
Y=\partial_y-\frac x2\partial_t,\qquad T=\partial_t.
]
Then
[
\theta(X)=\theta(Y)=0,\quad [X,Y]=-T,\quad [T,X]=[T,Y]=0,
\quad d\theta(X,Y)=1.
]
The coframe is ((\theta,dx,dy)), dual to ((T,X,Y)), and the volume form is
[
dV=\theta\wedge dx\wedge dy.
]

All signs below depend on this convention. Reversing the sign of (	heta), changing the Darboux convention, or changing the orientation changes corresponding displayed signs.

## 3. Quotient bundles in dimension three

Let (I^*\) be the differential ideal generated by (	heta), equivalently by (	heta) and (d\theta). Let
[
J^k=\{\omega\in\Omega^k(M):\omega\wedge\theta=0,
\;\omega\wedge d\theta=0\}.
]
The Rumin bundles in dimension three are
[
E^0=\Omega^0(M),\quad
E^1=\Omega^1(M)/I^1,\quad
E^2=J^2,\quad E^3=J^3.
]
Here (I^1=\theta\wedge\Omega^0). In degree two, (J^2) consists of forms of the form
[
p\,\theta\wedge dx+q\,\theta\wedge dy.
]
In degree three, (J^3=\Omega^3(M)).

The complex has the form
[
\Omega^0\xrightarrow{d_H}E^1
\xrightarrow{D_R}E^2
\xrightarrow{d_H}E^3.
]
The degree-zero and degree-two arrows are induced by exterior differentiation on the quotient/annihilator bundles. The middle arrow requires a corrected representative.

## 4. Unique corrected representative and local formula

Represent a class in (E^1) by its horizontal representative
[
\alpha=a\,dx+b\,dy.
]
Every other representative in that class is (\alpha+f\theta). There is a unique scalar function (f) such that
[
\beta=\alpha+f\theta,\qquad
d\beta\in J^2.
]
In dimension three, the defining condition is (\theta\wedge d\beta=0). Since
[
d\beta=d\alpha+df\wedge\theta+f\,d\theta
]
and (\theta\wedge df\wedge\theta=0), it becomes
[
\theta\wedge d\alpha+f\,\theta\wedge d\theta=0.
]
The contact condition makes (\theta\wedge d\theta) nowhere zero, so this equation determines (f) uniquely.

For the chosen Darboux frame,
[
d\alpha=(Xb-Ya)\,dx\wedge dy
 +(Ta)\,\theta\wedge dx
 +(Tb)\,\theta\wedge dy.
]
Consequently,
[
\boxed{f=Ya-Xb.}
]
The middle differential is defined by
[
D_R[\alpha]=d\beta=d(\alpha+f\theta).
]
Substitution gives the explicit formula
[
\boxed{
D_R[\alpha]
=(Ta-Xf)\,\theta\wedge dx
 +(Tb-Yf)\,\theta\wedge dy,
\qquad f=Ya-Xb.
}
]
Equivalently, in components relative to the basis
((\theta\wedge dx,\theta\wedge dy)),
[
D_R(a,b)=
\begin{pmatrix}
T a-X(Y a-X b)\\
T b-Y(Y a-X b)
\end{pmatrix}.
]
This is a differential operator of ordinary order two. Its Heisenberg/homogeneous order is four when horizontal derivatives have weight one and (T) has weight two.

**Why this is intrinsic:** the formula is a coordinate expression for the following intrinsic construction: take the class in (\Omega^1/I^1), choose its unique representative (\beta) satisfying (d\beta\in J^2), then apply (d). The Darboux formula computes that unique representative in a local trivialization; it does not define the construction independently of the contact structure.

## 5. The two outer differentials

For a function (u),
[
d_H^0u=[du],\qquad
d_H^0u=(Xu,Yu)
]
in the horizontal frame.

For a degree-two form
[
\gamma=p\,\theta\wedge dx+q\,\theta\wedge dy,
]
direct exterior differentiation gives
[
\boxed{d_H^2\gamma=(Yp-Xq)\,dV.}
]
These are first-order horizontal operators. Together with the second-order middle operator they give the mixed-order Rumin complex.

## 6. Chain identities

### 6.1 (d_H^0d_H^0=0)

The first (d_H^0u) is represented by the horizontal form
[
\alpha=(Xu)dx+(Yu)dy.
]
Its correction coefficient is
[
f=Y(Xu)-X(Yu)=[Y,X]u=Tu,
]
because ([X,Y]=-T). Thus the normalized representative is
[
\beta=\alpha+(Tu)\theta=du.
]
Therefore
[
D_R(d_H^0u)=d(du)=0.
]
This proves (D_Rd_H^0=0).

### 6.2 (d_H^2D_R=0)

By definition (D_R[\alpha]=d\beta). Since (d^2=0),
[
d_H^2(D_R[\alpha])=d(d\beta)=0.
]
This proves (d_H^2D_R=0).

### 6.3 (D_R) is well-defined on the quotient

If a representative is changed by (h\theta), it remains in the same class in (E^1). The normalization condition determines one and only one corrected representative. Hence the normalized (\beta), and therefore (d\beta), depends only on the class. This is the quotient-level well-definedness statement.

### 6.4 (d_H^2d_H^2=0)

This is the restriction of (d^2=0) to (J^2). It follows directly from exterior differentiation.

## 7. Formal adjoints in the left-invariant model

Equip the Heisenberg chart/model with the left-invariant metric for which
((\theta,dx,dy)) is orthonormal and use (dV=\theta\wedge dx\wedge dy). The vector fields (X,Y,T) are divergence-free for Haar volume, so
[
X^*=-X,\qquad Y^*=-Y,\qquad T^*=-T.
]

For (d_H^0u=(Xu,Yu)),
[
(d_H^0)^*(p,q)=-Xp-Yq.
]
For (d_H^2(p,q)=Yp-Xq),
[
(d_H^2)^*h=(-Yh,Xh).
]

Writing (C=Y a-X b), the middle operator is
[
D_R(a,b)=(Ta- XC,Tb-YC).
]
Its formal adjoint in this same frame is
[
oxed{
D_R^*(u,v)=
\left(
(-T+YX)u-Y^2v,;
X^2u+(-T+XY)v
\right).
}
]
This formula follows by reversing operator order under adjunction and using (X^*=-X), (Y^*=-Y), (T^*=-T). It is a formal local adjoint; global domains, self-adjoint extensions, and spectral properties require a specified compact quotient and operator domain.

No identity such as (D_R^*=-*D_R*) is assumed here. Such a statement must be checked against the precise Hodge star, degree conventions, bundle metrics, and sign convention in the source being compared.

## 8. Intertwining maps and comparison with Rumin–Seshadri/Kitaoka

There are two distinct comparison questions.

### 8.1 Intrinsic Rumin complex versus its corrected-representative realization

Let
[
q:\Omega^1\to\Omega^1/I^1
]
be the quotient map, and let (s:E^1\to\Omega^1) send a class to its unique normalized representative. Then
[
q\circ s=\operatorname{id}_{E^1},\qquad
D_R=d\circ s.
]
The map (s) is a right inverse of the quotient map, but it is a differential operator of order one in the chosen contact splitting; it is not an order-zero bundle map. The identity (D_R=d\circ s) is the exact intertwining statement for the middle arrow.

The outer arrows are induced by (d) on the quotient and on (J^2). The chain relations in Section 6 give the commutative-complex property.

### 8.2 Rumin–Seshadri and Kitaoka normalizations

Rumin–Seshadri use the contact complex and its associated Laplacians/torsion. Kitaoka's sphere calculations use additional geometric and representation-theoretic data. An equality of the underlying contact-complex construction does not by itself identify:
- the (L^2) inner products;
- the normalization of the contact form and Reeb period;
- the CR structure and its orientation;
- the flat coefficient bundle and unitary representation;
- the Laplacian sign and adjoint conventions;
- the determinant-line and analytic-torsion conventions.

The local map (s) above is not, by itself, a proof of a unitary equivalence between Kitaoka's spectral operators and the Rumin–Seshadri Laplacians. To establish that stronger result, one must write the bundle identifications and verify the operator diagram, including the metric adjoints. No such global spectral intertwiner is claimed in this file.

## 9. The quotient (S^3/2I): what follows and what remains

Let (S^3\subset\mathbb C^2) carry its standard CR/contact structure. The standard (SU(2)) action on (mathbb C^2) preserves the sphere, its standard contact distribution, and its CR structure. The binary icosahedral group (2I\subset SU(2)) acts freely on (S^3) by left multiplication, so the contact and CR structures descend to the smooth quotient (S^3/2I).

The Hopf circle action is by right multiplication and commutes with the left (SU(2)) action. Therefore it descends to the quotient and supplies the Seifert circle action. The base is the corresponding spherical orbifold quotient, with icosahedral signature ((2,3,5)). This identifies the geometric setting needed to investigate the three-dimensional CR-Seifert hypotheses.

The fundamental group of the quotient is (2I). A finite-dimensional unitary representation
[
\rho:2I\to U(r)
]
defines a flat unitary coefficient bundle on the quotient. The trivial representation is one case; nontrivial unitary twists must be treated separately in the spectral multiplicities and determinant formulas.

**Important limitation:** the local calculation above does not prove the analytic-torsion identity for any twist. Applying the Rumin–Seshadri theorem requires checking its exact CR-Seifert, metric, representation, and acyclicity/zero-mode conventions. Matching the resulting operators to Kitaoka's sphere formulas additionally requires descent through the (2I)-action and an explicit comparison of normalizations.
